Development trends
==================


This clause analyses the direction of technical, regulatory and market development. The standardization implications that follow from these trends are consolidated in clause 13.

Technical trends
----------------


Technical work in agentic AI security is moving away from the alignment of model weights and toward runtime control of agent behaviour. Attention turns to what an agent does while operating, rather than only to how its underlying model was trained. Identity and delegation increasingly form part of the safety argument, since an agent that cannot be authenticated cannot be held accountable, and one that presents no verifiable delegation credentials cannot be trusted to act on behalf of another party. Drift measurement and control planes are emerging as a distinct technical discipline, concerned with divergence between intended and observed agent behaviour over time rather than with traditional model drift.

Tool and plugin ecosystems create supply-chain risk of a different character from that of conventional software dependencies, because an agent invokes external capabilities at runtime and inherits their behaviour. Multi-agent orchestration is moving from experimental prototypes into production, with architectures becoming distributed and heterogeneous. On-device agents impose their own constraints, as terminal fragmentation and uneven security capabilities shape what can be assumed. Evaluation and assurance practice is maturing from ad hoc testing toward systematic method, with indicators mapped to agent capabilities and repeatable testing procedures.

Regulatory trends
-----------------


Agent-specific guidance is beginning to appear alongside general AI instruments. Early regulatory attention addresses AI systems broadly, while newer material starts to distinguish agents that plan, invoke tools and act with some autonomy. Human-oversight models are becoming objects of compliance rather than design aspirations, so the mechanisms of oversight, their triggers and their records attract specification. Interoperability and mutual recognition are becoming practical requirements for cross-border deployment, because an agent developed under one regime and deployed under another faces divergent expectations.

Incident reporting for agentic systems is in active policy development, with limited technical specification behind it, and existing regimes for ICT security provide only partial templates. Liability frameworks for autonomous agent actions remain underdeveloped relative to the pace of deployment, and the accountability chain across multiple agents, tools and operators stays difficult to establish. The direction of travel is toward oversight, reporting and liability concepts that assume delegated machine action rather than direct human operation.

Market and industry trends
--------------------------


A deployment-governance gap persists and may be widening, as agentic capabilities advance faster than the governance mechanisms inside deploying organisations. Platform-level agent identity is consolidating around a small number of architectural approaches, which shapes how credentials, permissions and audit trails are represented. Guardrail product categories are proliferating without common definitions, so runtime policy engines, content filters, tool-call interceptors, drift monitors and accountability records overlap in scope and naming. Harmonisation pressure across standards bodies is increasing as the number of agentic AI security work items grows, and the absence of shared terminology and reference models becomes more costly as the field expands.
