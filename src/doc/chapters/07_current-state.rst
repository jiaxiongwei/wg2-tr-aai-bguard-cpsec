Current state: landscape, practice and results
==============================================


Landscape, practice and reported results
----------------------------------------


This clause covers the current landscape, industrial practice and reported results that bear on behavioural guardrails for cyber-physical systems. Appendix III provides the documented events, cases and regulatory instruments as references.

Regulatory and policy instruments that bear on agentic AI place duties on deployers and providers, covering oversight, impact assessment and incident reporting, yet they rarely specify technical guardrail requirements, so a gap persists between legal duty and implementable control. Studies and reported results supply quantified evidence on guardrail performance, including attack success rates for prompt injection and related adversarial techniques. Outcomes, however, vary with attack type, agent architecture and deployment context, so differing test conditions and evaluation methods mean that comparisons across sources call for care. Industry practice treats agent identity, delegated authorisation and behavioural conformance as distinct layers of control, with documented work concentrating on identity and authorisation mechanisms while conformance checking of agent behaviour remains the least standardised. Across those sources the recurring finding is that taxonomy and framework work has advanced faster than operational guardrail specification. Standards bodies and industry consortia have produced substantial guidance on AI risk management, trust frameworks, evaluation methods and security requirements. The specification of enforceable behavioural guardrails for agentic systems, however, remains fragmented and immature.

Model-level and training-time guardrails
----------------------------------------


Model-level guardrails are embedded in the model through training and alignment procedures rather than imposed externally at runtime. Alignment training methods include reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF). These methods shape the model’s propensity to produce outputs consistent with human or AI-evaluated preferences, and they act mainly as a deterrent, reducing the likelihood that the model produces harmful or policy-violating content at all. Constitutional AI approaches extend this by providing a set of principles against which the model evaluates and revises its own outputs during training, thereby instilling refusal behaviour for certain classes of requests. Instruction-hierarchy training teaches the model to prioritise instructions from more trusted sources over those from less trusted sources, which serves as a deterrent against prompt-injection attacks that attempt to override system-level directives. Representation-level intervention methods, sometimes characterised as activation steering or representation engineering, operate on internal model states to suppress or amplify particular behavioural tendencies; these are typically applied at inference time but are derived from training-time analysis and function as deterrent controls.

Guard classifier models, often deployed as input and output safeguards, provide a separate learned layer that examines prompts and completions for policy violations. These classifiers are detective in nature: they monitor inputs and outputs against a policy and can trigger corrective action by blocking a response or re-routing a request. Their effectiveness against adaptive attacks is limited. An adversary who can observe or infer the classifier’s decision boundary may craft inputs that evade detection, and the guard classifier itself may be vulnerable to the same class of adversarial perturbations that affect the primary model. Model-level and training-time guardrails therefore provide a baseline of deterrent and detective capability, but they do not constitute a complete control set, and their limits against adaptive adversaries are significant.

Input and context guardrails
----------------------------


Input and context guardrails operate on the data that enters the agent’s reasoning process. Prompt filtering and injection detection mechanisms examine user inputs and retrieved content for patterns associated with prompt-injection attacks, such as instructions embedded in unexpected locations or attempts to override system-level directives. These mechanisms serve a deterrent role when they block suspicious inputs before they reach the model, and a detective role when they flag inputs for logging or human review without blocking. Instruction and data separation is a design principle in which the agent’s treatment of system instructions is architecturally distinguished from its treatment of user-provided or retrieved data, so that the latter is not interpreted as instructions. This separation is deterrent in effect: it reduces the attack surface for injection by constraining the pathways through which adversarial text can influence behaviour.

Provenance and trust tagging of context assigns metadata to each piece of retrieved or supplied information indicating its source and the level of trust that the agent is permitted to place in it. Retrieval filtering applies policy constraints to the documents or passages that a retrieval-augmented generation system is allowed to return, excluding content from untrusted or unverified sources. Untrusted-content marking provides an explicit signal to the model or to downstream guardrails that particular context is not treated as authoritative. These provenance and trust mechanisms are primarily deterrent: they prevent untrusted content from being treated as equivalent to trusted instructions. Their detective value lies in the audit trail they create, which supports post-incident analysis of how adversarial content entered the reasoning chain.

Behavioural and action guardrails
---------------------------------


Behavioural and action guardrails constrain what the agent is permitted to do in the world, as distinct from what it is permitted to say. Goal locking fixes the agent’s objective for the duration of a task and prevents the objective from being modified by user input, retrieved content, or the agent’s own reasoning. Task, step, token, and cost budgets impose quantitative limits on the resources an agent consumes in pursuit of its objective. These budgets are deterrent when enforced prospectively (the agent cannot exceed the budget by construction) and detective when they trigger alerts upon nearing or exceeding thresholds. In gateway-based deployments, session caps are applied at the AI gateway, and automated blocks halt downstream execution when an agent exceeds its assigned budget multiplier.

Action allowlists and typed tool schemas define the set of actions the agent is permitted to invoke and the precise argument types each action accepts. An allowlist is deterrent because actions outside the list are rejected; a typed schema is deterrent because malformed or unexpected arguments are rejected before execution. Argument validation examines the values passed to a tool call against policy constraints, providing a further deterrent layer. Least-agency scoping limits the permissions associated with each tool to the minimum necessary for the task at hand, following the principle that excessive agency expands the radius of harm when the agent is compromised. Confirmation gates for irreversible or high-impact actions require explicit human approval before the action executes, implementing a human-in-the-loop pattern for actions such as deleting data, sending communications to external parties, or executing financial transactions. These gates are corrective in the sense that they interrupt an action that would otherwise proceed autonomously, but their primary function is deterrent: the requirement for approval discourages the agent from attempting such actions without justification. Sandboxed execution and egress control constrain the environment in which the agent’s actions take effect, preventing the agent from reaching network resources or system capabilities outside its designated boundary. Rate and resource limits bound the frequency and intensity of agent actions, preventing runaway behaviour that could exhaust downstream systems.

Monitoring, detection and drift control
---------------------------------------


Monitoring and detection guardrails provide visibility into agent behaviour and identify deviations from expected or authorised conduct. Policy-versus-behaviour drift measurement compares the operator the agentic system actually uses with the operator declared by policy, and treats any divergence between the two as a signal that needs attention. This measurement is inherently detective: it observes behaviour and assesses it against a declared standard. Anomaly detection over action traces examines sequences of tool calls, state changes, and decisions for patterns that deviate from a baseline established for the agent’s task and environment. Memory-integrity monitoring checks that the agent’s memory and context stores have not been corrupted by adversarial inputs or unauthorised modifications. Inter-agent trust verification provides a mechanism for one agent to assess whether another agent’s assertions or actions warrant trust. Risk scoring aggregates these detective signals into a quantitative assessment that can drive escalation or intervention decisions. The monitoring layer is primarily detective, but it also serves a corrective function when its outputs trigger automated interventions, such as reducing an agent’s autonomy tier or suspending a particular tool call pattern.

Intervention, recovery and accountability
-----------------------------------------


Intervention and recovery guardrails act after a deviation has been detected or a harm has begun to materialise. Circuit breakers and kill switches provide the ability to halt an agent’s execution, sever its network connections, and invalidate its access tokens. These are corrective controls of last resort. Rollback and compensation of unauthorised state changes attempt to undo actions that the agent has taken outside its authorised scope. This is technically challenging, because many actions in external systems are not reversible, and rollback in practice works only for systems that provide compensating operations or transactional semantics. Escalation and human takeover transfer control of an ongoing task from the agent to a human operator, implementing a dynamic transition from autonomous to supervised operation when the risk tier or detected anomalies warrant it. Structured forensic logging records the agent’s reasoning chain, tool invocations, and state changes in a tamper-evident form, supporting post-incident investigation and regulatory compliance. Accountability manifests may bind agent identity to operator-declared intent and operational scope, and provide intent verification procedures that compare observed behaviour against declared intent, thereby supporting both detective and corrective functions. These intervention and accountability mechanisms are corrective in primary role, with the accountability manifest also serving a detective function through its verification procedures.

Identity and authorization guardrails
-------------------------------------


Identity and authorisation guardrails govern the credentials and permissions under which an agent acts. Task-scoped and time-limited credentials ensure that an agent’s access tokens are valid only for a specific task and expire after a defined interval, reducing the window of opportunity for misuse if a token is compromised. Delegation artefacts with verifiable provenance record the chain of delegation from a principal to an agent, enabling downstream systems to assess whether the agent’s action is within the scope of authority that has been delegated to it. Least privilege limits the permissions associated with an agent identity to the minimum necessary for its assigned task, consistent with the broader least-agency principle at the action layer. Revocable independent agent identities provide each agent with a distinct identity that can be revoked without affecting other agents, enabling containment of a compromised agent through identity revocation rather than broader system shutdown. These identity and authorisation controls are predominantly deterrent: they prevent an agent from acting outside its authorised scope by construction, and they limit the impact of a compromise by constraining what a compromised identity can do. Their detective value lies in the audit records they generate, which show which identity performed which action under which delegation of authority.
